A MEMORIAL IN ICE, TO ICE

MORAINE

Grinnell Glacier, Montana. First measured at 710 acres.
Scroll forward in time — the ice on this page melts exactly as much as the record says it did.

▼

1850 — The baseline

Surveyors sketch a river of ice pressing against the Garden Wall. It has been here, grinding rock into flour, since before there was anyone to name it.

1920 — The hotel era

Tourists ride mules up to touch it. Postcards call it eternal. The ice has already signed different paperwork.

1966 — The first aerial audit

USGS maps it properly from the sky: less than half the baseline remains. The word "glacier" starts doing quiet overtime on the park's signage.

1993 — The measured decline

Repeat photography makes the retreat undeniable — the same tripod holes, the same mountain, a third less ice in every frame.

2005 — The lake grows

Where ice was, there is now turquoise water — Upper Grinnell Lake, beautiful the way a receipt is detailed. Melt has a color, and people photograph it.

Now — Still here, smaller

Roughly a sixth of the baseline remains, huddled in the mountain's shadow. The forecasts disagree only about the date of the last acre.

Figures follow the published USGS area series (approximate; the 1920 and 2026 values are interpolated from adjacent measurements).

A moraine is the pile of rock a glacier leaves behind —
the receipt for everything it carried.
